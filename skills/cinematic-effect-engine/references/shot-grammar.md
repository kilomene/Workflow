# Shot Grammar: Hierarchy, Movement, Lighting, Composition, Continuity

Detailed camera-language reference for constructing individual shots and scenes.
Pairs with `camera-angle-bible.md` (specific angles/heights) and
`shot-prompt-checklist.md` (quick per-shot fill-in).

## Shot hierarchy

Begin a new scene with visual geography before emotion — establish where we are
and who's there before pushing in on how anyone feels. A typical scene moves
through some or all of these in order, though not every scene needs all seven:

1. **Establishing shot** — reveal the location
2. **Wide shot** — show character placement within that location
3. **Medium shot** — conversation, body-language-level framing
4. **Close-up** — emotion on a face
5. **Extreme close-up** — a critical, high-stakes detail (eyes, a trembling hand)
6. **Insert shot** — an important object the story needs the audience to register
7. **Final reveal** — a transition beat or cliffhanger framing to close the scene

Don't force all seven into every scene — a short dialogue beat might only need
2-4. But don't open on a close-up or medium shot when the audience hasn't been
oriented to the location yet, and don't repeat the same framing (e.g. three
medium shots in a row) excessively; vary the hierarchy position to keep visual
rhythm.

## Camera movement

Use only purposeful movement — every movement choice should be justified by what
it does for the story beat of that shot, not used as decoration.

- **Dolly in** — rising tension
- **Dolly out** — loneliness or defeat
- **Tracking** — follow action
- **Push-in** — emotional realization
- **Crane up** — reveal scale
- **Static frame** — power and suspense (stillness is a deliberate choice, not a
  default)
- **Shoulder follow** — intimate, kinetic pursuit of a character
- **Slow reveal pan** — building tension before a reveal
- **Handheld** — urgency, chaos, subjectivity (use only when the scene's emotional
  register calls for instability, not as a texture default)

Avoid constant movement — if every shot in a scene moves, none of the movement
reads as meaningful. A static frame used deliberately (e.g. to hold on a
confrontation) is often stronger than reflexive motion.

## Lighting

Motivated practical lighting only — every light source in a shot should trace
back to something plausibly in the scene:

- Window light
- Street lights
- Lamps
- Neon
- Moonlight
- Fire
- Vehicle headlights

No flat, artificial, overexposed "AI lighting" with no visible or implied source.
If a shot needs a specific mood, achieve it by choosing which practical source
dominates (e.g. cold moonlight vs warm lamp light) rather than adding generic
ambient brightening.

## Composition

Apply real cinematographic composition principles, chosen per shot rather than
defaulted to one look:

- Rule of thirds
- Leading lines
- Foreground depth (something in the near-frame to create layered depth)
- Negative space (used deliberately, e.g. for isolation or loneliness)
- Symmetry — only when it's dramatically justified (formality, control, unease),
  not as a default framing habit
- Layered backgrounds — avoid a flat single-plane background; give the frame
  depth through foreground/midground/background elements

Characters should never feel pasted onto the environment — lighting direction,
shadow behavior, and scale must be consistent between subject and background so
the composite reads as one coherent physical space.

## Continuity within a scene

Unless the script intentionally changes perspective, maintain across all shots in
one continuous scene:
- Identical camera height logic (don't jump from knee-height to overhead without
  a reason)
- Consistent screen direction (a character moving left-to-right should keep doing
  so unless a deliberate direction change is motivated by the geography)
- Consistent lighting direction (the key light source's apparent direction
  shouldn't flip between shots of the same moment)
- Consistent lens/focal-length logic (don't swing from a wide establishing lens
  feel to an extreme telephoto compression within the same continuous beat
  without reason)

## Forbidden by default

Unless the user explicitly asks for one of these as a stylistic choice, avoid:
- Random zooms
- Hyperactive camera shake
- Drone movement indoors without a clear justification
- Fish-eye distortion
- Unnaturally shallow "cartoon" depth of field that doesn't match real lens
  behavior
- Unmotivated whip pans
- Repeating the identical angle/framing shot after shot within a scene
